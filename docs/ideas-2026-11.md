## 2. A fuller feature list (beyond ADR 0018's eleven)

Ranked by how much each is likely to add to "realism as interplay"
(memory: realism-is-interplay), roughest cost first. All are ideas for the owner.

1. **Druse faces in open cavities.** Quartz-lined stones show crystal tips pointing
   into the hollow; agatesim grows quartz grains already, but the cavity's surface is
   smooth. Needs option A first. Cost: moderate (3D tips on grains).
2. **Host rind and the contact.** The owner already listed rind, host speckle and
   pigment bleed into host (HANDOFF "later"). A thin altered rind (often green or
   brown) frames most faces in photos.
3. **Plumes and moss** (next on the owner's list): dendritic or tufted inclusions;
   agatesim has a dendrite mode the renderer refuses ("inclusions other than tubes").
   So the render side needs the same treatment tonight gave the layers.
4. **Depth through clear bands** (on the owner's list): seen only in renders, never in
   stand-ins, because it is light passing through. Tonight's renderer work makes it
   testable on variety stones.
5. **Needle inclusions** (sagenite-like): straight fibres crossing bands, a common
   "odd inclusion" in collector photos. Cheap as a layer (like cracks: lines in 3D).
6. **White opaque replacements** (calcite or zeolite remnants): chalky patches that
   break bands. Cheap as a layer (like mottle, white instead of red).
7. **Eyes** (orbicular rings on the outer bands) and **escape tubes** (already queued).
8. **Saw marks and polish level** ("glints and finish", last by the owner's order):
   matters for the photo look, not the stone.

## 3. Engineering ideas (code quality and speed)

- **Band roughness costs about 7 x per point** (its frame reads growth time seven
  times). A coarse cached grid of the growth-time gradient would cut renders of rough
  stones by a large factor; measure first (memory: measure before building).
- **One colour model.** Stand-ins use agatesim's colours; renders use the optics.
  They disagree on red stones (option B). A preview made from the optics' single-
  scattering colour would let previews predict renders, so the owner's eye on sheets
  carries over to renders.
- **variety.py is ~1,650 lines** holding recipes, drawing, layers and checks. Split
  into recipe, cracks, mottle, checks (no behaviour change; tests guard it).
- **Window cuts** (`nodule.sheet.window_cut`) belong in `Specimen` beside `cut`.

## 4. Next-phase projects that build on Nodule

Each reuses what Nodule has (procedural stones with known truth, a renderer, photo
descriptors, a machine compare). Ordered by how directly they serve the owner's aims.

1. **Bands as signals, tested where the answer is known.** The owner sees band
   irregularity as a possible environmental signal (ADR 0014 memory). Synthetic
   stones with a *known* forcing let one measure how much of it a cut face can
   recover, and how resolution and cut angle censor it (ADR 0006): a test of the
   inversion before it meets real stones. Natural extension: other banded archives
   whose layers are dated, as a check on the method rather than on agates.
2. **A labelled dataset for counting bands beyond the eye.** Photos censor band
   counts (ADR 0006 s.10). Renders come with exact labels (every boundary, crack and
   dust line), so a model trained on them could estimate counts and spacing below
   the visible limit, then be checked against the owner's 30-photo notes.
3. **Photo to recipe.** Given a photo, estimate the recipe that makes the closest
   stone (outline, band density, warp, colour, features present): a "what am I
   looking at" aid, and a way to set sheet shares from the library's photos instead of
   assumptions (memory: library without owner labour).
4. **The cutter's choice as a tool.** Nodule already chooses cuts (clean cuts, avoiding
   cracks). Given a 3D stone (one day from CT), rank planes by how much of what the
   owner values each would show.
5. **An interactive slicer** (a web page): drag a plane through a synthetic stone and
   see the face change; good for explaining why one stone shows such different faces.
6. **Other banded stones** with the same machinery: malachite, rhodochrosite, banded
   jaspers. Mostly palette and growth-front changes; a test of how general the
   approach is.
